To caption an IRL replay for YouTube, finish the video edit first. Then choose
whether captions should be part of the picture or a separate track viewers can
turn on and off. VISP Typography can transcribe an uploaded video, let you edit
word-level captions, and export a captioned MP4. For a separate YouTube caption
track, create a timed file such as SRT and add it in YouTube Studio.

That choice matters when a two-hour Twitch or Kick walk becomes a fifteen-minute
YouTube video. You may want to correct a street name, translate a conversation,
or let viewers hide the text. Do the caption work against the final edit, not
the original broadcast timeline. This guide covers replay uploads, not live
YouTube caption setup.

## Choose burned-in captions or a YouTube track

Burned-in captions are pixels in the video. Viewers cannot hide them or select
another language track. A separate caption track stays independent of the
picture, so viewers can enable it and YouTube can associate it with a language.

VISP Typography creates a captioned MP4. It does not export an SRT file. Choose
it when you want styled text permanently visible in the finished video. Choose
a timed subtitle file when you need YouTube's player controls or separate
language tracks. You can use both, but turn off one layer while reviewing so
duplicate text does not obscure the picture.

VISP's phone live subtitles are also burned into the camera image. They remain
in any recording made from that source. The [live-caption guide](/blog/live-captions-phone-irl-stream-obs)
explains that tradeoff; VISP's [media architecture documentation](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/architecture)
describes how the camera feed reaches OBS.

## Finish the video edit first

Make the version you intend to publish. Remove waiting screens, choose the
walk segments, set the final audio mix, and complete any speed changes. Export
the video and use that exact version as the reference for captions.

For example, a guest starts speaking at 12:00 in the stream recording. You
remove the first two minutes and another thirty-second pause before the guest.
In the replay, that speech starts at 09:30. Captions timed to the original
recording now arrive two and a half minutes late. One global offset will not
fix later cuts or speed changes.

Keep the recording, editing project, final video, and any caption file
together. Use matching version names, such as `harbor-walk-v3.mp4` and
`harbor-walk-v3.en.srt`. This is a simple way to avoid pairing a caption file
with the wrong export.

Listen to the exported audio rather than relying on the editor preview. Check
that your voice, guests, and studio commentary are present. If speech is
doubled or a microphone is delayed, use the [phone audio troubleshooting guide](/blog/fix-phone-audio-obs)
before the next session. Caption edits cannot fix the sound in the video.

## Create a captioned MP4 with VISP Typography

VISP Typography accepts MP4, QuickTime MOV, and WebM uploads. It creates
word-level captions in English or Finnish. You can correct words and their
start and end times, choose a caption style, adjust its position, and export
an H.264 MP4 with the captions rendered into the video.

Check the framing before choosing this route: the current Typography export
reframes video to vertical 1080 × 1920 (9:16). A landscape replay will be
cropped. If you need to preserve the original widescreen frame, use an editor
that keeps that aspect ratio and add a separate timed caption track in YouTube
Studio instead.

1. Finish and export the replay from your video editor.
2. Open [VISP Typography](https://typography.visp-stream.com) and sign in.
3. Upload the final MP4, MOV, or WebM file and select the spoken language.
4. Review the generated transcript against the video. Correct names and
   mistakes, then check word timing around edits.
5. Adjust the style and position so the text stays readable without covering
   faces, signs, or important parts of the scene.
6. Export the MP4 and watch the exported file from beginning to end.

Speech recognition is a starting point, not a proofread transcript. Listen for
street names, guest names, channel names, negatives, numbers, and overlapping
voices. Do not fill in words that traffic or wind made impossible to hear.
Check the whole video at normal speed after editing. A transcript can look
correct while a word appears too early, stays on screen through a cut, or
misses a quiet sentence. Fix the text and timing in the editor, then export
again. The MP4 keeps the selected style in the picture, so review it on a small
screen and make sure the captions remain legible over both bright streets and
dark scenes. Keep a clean copy of the original export so you can change the
caption design later without stacking another caption layer on top.

## Add a separate caption track in YouTube Studio

If viewers should control captions separately from the picture, prepare a timed
caption file against the final export. YouTube supports basic SubRip `.srt`
files as plain UTF-8 text; SRT styling markup is not recognized. Check
[YouTube's supported caption formats](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2734698?hl=en)
if your editor offers several export choices.

Follow YouTube's [caption upload instructions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2734796?hl=en):

1. Open YouTube Studio and select **Subtitles**.
2. Select the finished video and add the intended language.
3. Choose to add a caption file for that language.
4. Choose **Upload file**, then **With timing** for a timed SRT file.
5. Select the file and save it.

If you use automatic captions as a draft, review them before publishing.
YouTube notes that background noise, overlapping speech, and poor sound can
affect recognition. It generates new automatic captions for a completed live
replay through its VOD process; those captions may differ from the live
captions. See YouTube's [automatic-caption guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en).

## Review every cut and the final export

Watch the opening with captions enabled. Confirm the first sentence appears
with the first audible words, not during a title card or waiting screen. Then
check the passages immediately after each cut, plus sections where you changed
speed or replaced audio.

If captions are early or late by the same amount throughout, inspect the
starting offset. If timing matches at the beginning and fails after a cut,
compare the caption file with the edit version. If the error grows during an
unchanged section, inspect how the timings were generated. Avoid nudging lines
one by one until you know which timing problem you have.

Check the last spoken sentence too. Watch a representative passage on a phone
at normal speed and once with sound off. Confirm the words are readable and
the speaker is clear. For a separate YouTube track, turn captions off and
confirm only the intended permanent graphics remain. For a burned-in export,
check that no old live overlay creates a second set of words.

## Plan the next recording for reuse

If you regularly turn streams into videos, decide how you will record before
the next broadcast. A phone subtitle overlay is part of that camera picture.
A clean replay needs a recording without that overlay; recording the same
captioned source elsewhere will not remove it.

Use the [VISP phone setup documentation](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/phone-app)
to prepare the camera feed. For edited videos, try [VISP Typography](https://typography.visp-stream.com)
with a short recording first, then review the exported MP4 before captioning a
full afternoon of IRL footage.
