If your phone overheats while sending an IRL camera to OBS, fix the **thermal
budget**, not one mystery setting. Start at 720p30, keep the phone shaded and
ventilated, reduce display brightness, avoid stacking other demanding jobs on
the device, and test the charging plan for the full length of the broadcast.
Keep a local fallback scene ready in OBS so you can stop the camera safely if
the operating system shows a temperature warning.

A warm phone is not automatically failing. Apple says camera apps, high-quality
video streaming, and wireless charging can normally make an iPhone warm.
Google lists high-definition recording, large uploads, mobile data, and doing
those jobs while charging among the reasons a Pixel may heat up. The production
problem begins when the device has to protect itself by limiting charging,
performance, the camera, the display, or the network connection.

## Understand what the phone is doing

In the standard VISP-to-OBS workflow, the field phone still performs the first
video encode. The VISP app captures the selected camera and microphone,
encodes H.264 video and AAC audio, and sends one contribution feed over SRT.
The relay authenticates and forwards that feed. OBS decodes it, builds the
finished program, and sends the Twitch or Kick broadcast from the home
computer.

That design keeps OBS scenes, browser sources, alerts, recording, and the
platform stream key off the phone. It does not make phone capture free. During
an outdoor broadcast, one device may be:

- running the camera and hardware video encoder continuously;
- keeping a bright preview and chat visible;
- uploading the contribution feed over a mobile radio;
- running maps, Bluetooth audio, or another production app;
- charging from a power bank;
- absorbing heat from the sun and warm air.

Each task may work alone. Heat becomes a problem when they run together faster
than the phone can release it.

Apple's current [temperature guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-euro/118431)
documents the resulting protections: charging can slow or stop, the display can
dim or go black, cellular signal can weaken as radios enter a low-power state,
camera features can be disabled, and some workloads can run at lower frame
rates. Google's [Pixel heat guidance](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/3333708?hl=en)
similarly says a hot phone may reduce CPU and charging performance, disable the
camera, limit mobile data or Wi-Fi, enter low-power mode, or shut down.

Those protections are safety features. Do not try to defeat them during a live
show.

## Start with a cooler video baseline

Use the lowest format that meets the production need, then move upward only
after a full-duration test. The VISP phone app prefers **720p at 30 fps** when
the selected camera supports it. That is a useful IRL baseline: it asks the
camera and encoder to process fewer pixels and frames than 1080p60, while OBS
can still place the feed inside a normal scene.

Do not increase resolution, frame rate, and bitrate together. Change one
variable, run the phone for the expected show duration, and observe whether the
device remains stable. A ten-minute indoor check cannot validate a two-hour
walk in afternoon sun.

Use this order:

1. begin at 720p30;
2. select a bitrate the real field upload can sustain with margin;
3. test camera motion, fine detail, and OBS composition;
4. raise resolution only when the picture genuinely needs it;
5. test the new setting outdoors for the full planned duration.

Lowering bitrate alone is not guaranteed to solve heat on every model. Camera
capture, scaling, frame rate, screen brightness, radios, ambient temperature,
and charging all remain in the budget. Treat each change as a measured
experiment, not a universal cure.

The [mobile IRL bitrate guide](/blog/best-bitrate-irl-streaming-phone-obs)
helps choose a contribution setting without confusing it with the separate OBS
upload to Twitch or Kick.

## Keep direct heat away and let warm air escape

Shade is the highest-value physical change because it removes an external heat
source without reducing picture quality. Position the operator, mount, or a
small sun hood so the phone body stays out of direct sunlight. Do not leave it
against a sun-heated window, vehicle dashboard, or dark enclosed bag between
shots.

Apple specifies an ambient operating range of 0–35°C for iPhone and warns that
extended camera use in hot conditions or direct sunlight can change device
performance. Google also says to keep a Pixel out of direct sunlight and away
from enclosed or poorly ventilated areas.

Build the rig around airflow:

- leave the back and edges of the phone open rather than pressed against fabric;
- avoid wrapping the phone and power bank together in one insulated pouch;
- keep the power bank separate so its heat does not warm the phone;
- check that the mount does not cover the whole rear surface;
- test the final rig, not the bare phone on a desk.

A purpose-built mount can be safer than improvising a sealed weather cover.
If rain protection is required, account for its loss of ventilation and test it
under the same weather and charging conditions.

## Reduce display and app load

The preview does not need maximum brightness in shade. Reduce brightness until
the operator can still judge framing, status, and chat safely. Google explicitly
recommends lowering display brightness when a Pixel gets too warm.

Remove avoidable foreground work too. Do not run a public Twitch or Kick
playback on the same phone just to confirm that OBS is live; that adds video
decoding and download activity while the device is already capturing and
uploading. Use VISP's stream status and chat view on the camera phone, and
check the public program from a second muted device when possible.

Navigation may be essential during an IRL stream, but it is still part of the
heat budget. Apple's guidance specifically names extended GPS navigation in
hot conditions. If the route needs maps, test camera, navigation, screen
brightness, Bluetooth, and charging together rather than validating each app
separately.

## Treat charging as another heat input

A power bank solves runtime, not cooling. Charging adds heat at the same time
the phone is capturing and transmitting. Apple lists wireless charging among
the normal causes of warmth, while Google calls out resource-heavy use during
charging and instructs users to disconnect power if the phone becomes too hot.

Use a practical power plan:

1. start with the phone charged rather than recovering a nearly empty battery
   at maximum speed during the show;
2. use a known-good cable and charger appropriate for the device;
3. keep the power bank physically separate from the phone;
4. rehearse while charging, because a battery-only test proves a different rig;
5. watch for a charging-paused or temperature warning;
6. if the device reports excessive heat, cut to fallback, stop capture, unplug
   it, move it to a cooler shaded place, and let it cool.

Do not promise an uninterrupted show by forcing the phone to keep charging.
Apple and Google both describe charging limits as part of the device's thermal
protection. Respecting that limit is safer than protecting the current shot.

## Tell heat apart from a bad network

Heat and network congestion can both look like a stuttering remote camera, but
the first checks differ.

| Symptom | Check first |
| --- | --- |
| The whole phone is hot, display dims, charging pauses, or an OS warning appears | Stop the camera and cool the device |
| VISP reports rising packet loss or RTT while the phone otherwise behaves normally | Lower bitrate or improve the route |
| Camera or frame rate becomes limited after a long warm run | Treat it as thermal protection |
| OBS drops frames on its platform connection while the phone feed is stable | Troubleshoot the home OBS uplink |
| The feed stops immediately when the app leaves the foreground | Return to the VISP app and keep it active |
| The phone feed stops but OBS and local sources continue | Use the fallback scene while the phone recovers |

Do not keep raising SRT latency to solve a hot device. SRT latency gives packet
retransmissions time to arrive; it does not cool the camera or encoder. The
[VISP encoder guide](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/broadcaster-setup)
explains how to tune that network recovery window from measured round-trip
time.

## Rehearse the failure, not only the happy path

Run a thermal rehearsal before an important Twitch or Kick broadcast:

1. assemble the exact mount, case, cable, power bank, microphone, and headset;
2. choose 720p30 and the planned bitrate;
3. start the phone feed and receive it in OBS;
4. run any chat, maps, and Bluetooth equipment you will use live;
5. place the rig in shade under realistic ambient conditions;
6. let it run for at least the planned segment length;
7. record whether the display dims, charging changes, the camera stutters, or
   the operating system warns;
8. stop and restart the phone feed, then confirm the same OBS source recovers;
9. force one failure and verify OBS switches to local fallback;
10. keep the coolest settings that still produce an acceptable picture.

VISP's generated OBS Media Source reconnects to the same device path after the
phone returns. The home OBS broadcast can remain live throughout, showing a
local slate, studio camera, recap video, or other planned scene. Follow the
[bad-mobile-network guide](/blog/keep-stream-live-bad-mobile-network) for the
full fallback and reconnection workflow.

## What to do during a temperature warning

Treat the warning as a stop signal:

1. switch OBS to the local fallback scene;
2. stop the VISP camera stream if the interface still responds;
3. disconnect charging;
4. move the phone away from sunlight into a cooler, ventilated place;
5. allow it to cool before restarting;
6. resume at a lower format or with fewer simultaneous tasks;
7. verify stable video and audio in OBS before cutting back.

Apple tells users who see its temperature warning to turn off the device, move
it to a cooler environment away from direct sunlight, and allow it to cool.
Google likewise says to unplug a phone that is too hot, move it somewhere
cooler, and stop using it until it cools down. Follow the instructions shown by
your own device rather than a live-production shortcut.

## Know what VISP changes—and what it does not

VISP keeps the phone as a contribution source and moves final production to
OBS. That removes the need to render OBS scenes, browser alerts, and the final
platform encode on the field phone. The Twitch or Kick stream key stays in OBS.

On the normal relay-to-OBS path, VISP **does not transcode** the contribution.
If the phone sends an unnecessarily demanding format, the relay will not turn
it into a cooler one. Change resolution, frame rate, and bitrate at the phone.

The relay also **does not bond network connections or combine their capacity
into a faster upload**. A native multi-link redundancy mode can send duplicate
SRT packets over Wi-Fi and cellular, but redundancy is not extra cooling or
aggregated throughput and may increase radio and data use. Leave it out of the
first thermal baseline, then test it separately only if the route requires that
resilience.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal for a phone to feel warm while streaming?

Yes. Continuous camera use, encoding, network upload, display use, and charging
can make a device warm. The actionable boundary is changed behavior or an
operating-system warning: dimming, paused charging, limited camera or radio
functions, reduced performance, or shutdown.

### Should I lower bitrate or resolution first?

Start with a complete 720p30 baseline and a bitrate the route can sustain.
Resolution and frame rate change camera and encoding work; bitrate changes the
encoder target and network load. The thermal result varies by phone, so change
one setting and repeat the same full-duration test.

### Is Wi-Fi always cooler than cellular?

Not always, but Google recommends Wi-Fi instead of mobile data when possible
for a Pixel that runs hot. For a moving IRL stream, stability and coverage still
matter. Test the actual radio, signal conditions, and route rather than assuming
the network label decides the temperature.

### Will sending the camera to OBS stop overheating?

It can reduce the work compared with building and distributing the whole show
on the phone, because OBS handles scenes and final output at home. The phone
still captures, encodes, displays, and uploads its contribution, so it still
needs a thermal plan.

### Should I keep streaming after the phone shows a temperature warning?

No. Switch OBS to fallback, follow the device's warning, disconnect power if
instructed, and let the phone cool before restarting.
