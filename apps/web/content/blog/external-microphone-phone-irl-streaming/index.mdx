An external microphone can make an IRL stream easier to understand, but merely
plugging one into a phone does not prove that the streaming app is using it.
The phone may keep its built-in microphone, a wireless receiver may change the
playback route, and a loud transmitter can clip before OBS ever sees the
signal.

The reliable workflow is short: **connect the microphone before capture,
select the named input in VISP, prove it with the phone meter, and judge a real
recording or platform test.** This guide applies whether the finished show goes
through a home OBS studio or VISP Direct to Twitch or Kick.

![An external microphone feeds the selected phone input, which VISP carries either into a home OBS mix or through Direct to a streaming destination](./external-mic-audio-path.svg)

## The short answer: verify the route, not the connector

Use these checkpoints in order:

1. Stop the VISP contribution if it is running.
2. Connect and power the microphone, receiver, or audio interface.
3. Open **Settings → Audio → Microphone** in the VISP phone app.
4. Choose the named input instead of assuming **System default** changed.
5. Speak close to that microphone and watch the phone's audio indicator.
6. Start a test and record it in OBS, or check the Direct output from a second
   device through headphones.
7. Disconnect and reconnect the accessory once before the real stream, then
   repeat the selection check.

Do not leave the final proof until viewers arrive. A connector can fit, a
receiver can show a green light, and the wrong microphone can still be active.

## Pick the simplest microphone route that meets the job

No connector type is automatically best for every IRL stream. Choose around
movement, weather, monitoring, and the sound you need to capture.

| Input route | Good fit | Check before going live |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phone's built-in microphone | A quick test or ambient scene where the phone stays near the speaker | Wind, handling noise, and changing distance |
| Wired lavalier or compact phone microphone | One nearby speaker with minimal equipment | Correct phone adapter, cable rub, strain relief, and whether the input appears by name |
| USB audio interface or plug-in wireless receiver | A microphone system with its own gain or a feed from a venue mixer | Power draw, adapter stability, input gain, mono/stereo layout, and what happens if the cable moves |
| Bluetooth headset microphone | Cable-free speech when the phone exposes the headset as an input | The actual selected input, return-audio route, battery, range, and reconnection behavior |

Follow the accessory maker's compatibility instructions for your exact phone.
A USB-C, Lightning, TRRS, or Bluetooth label alone does not guarantee that a
particular phone, adapter, and microphone combination exposes an input to
third-party capture apps.

For outdoor speech, microphone placement and wind protection usually matter
more than adding aggressive processing later. The
[iPhone wind and background-noise guide](/blog/reduce-wind-noise-iphone-irl-streaming)
explains that separate problem in detail.

## 1. Connect the microphone before opening its capture session

Mount the microphone and secure every adapter before starting the VISP stream.
If the accessory has a receiver or interface, power it on and wait until the
phone recognizes it. Then open VISP settings.

VISP lists the audio inputs currently reported by iOS or Android. On iPhone,
the app reads `AVAudioSession.availableInputs` and asks the system to use the
selected port. Apple's
[setPreferredInput documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/setpreferredinput%28_%3A%29)
is careful about the distinction: the method requests an input-route change,
and the current route is the way to determine whether it took effect.

Android has the same useful caution. VISP reads the available input devices
and requests the selected device from its microphone capture source. Android's
[AudioRouting reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/media/AudioRouting.html)
states that a preferred device is not guaranteed to be the device actually
used for recording. That is why the meter and listening test are part of the
setup, not optional troubleshooting.

Keep the stream stopped while changing the microphone. VISP deliberately
disables the picker during an active contribution so the capture session does
not change underneath a live Twitch or Kick show.

## 2. Select the named input and prove it is active

In **Settings → Audio → Microphone**, choose the accessory name when it is
available. Use **System default** only when you intentionally want the phone to
decide the route.

Then run a wrong-input test:

1. Hold the external microphone at its real on-air position.
2. Speak a normal sentence, then one louder sentence.
3. Move the external microphone farther away while keeping the phone still.
4. Briefly cover the phone's built-in microphone area without touching the
   external microphone.
5. Watch how the VISP audio indicator changes.

The meter should follow the microphone you selected. If it behaves as though
the phone itself is still listening, stop and reselect the input. If the named
device has disappeared, reconnect it, reopen settings, and check again.

The meter proves that sound reaches the capture path, but it cannot identify
distortion, radio interference, cable noise, or a hollow Bluetooth route.
Listen to a recording before deciding the setup is good.

## 3. Set gain before using the OBS fader

Speak at the loudest realistic level for the event. Include cheering, turning
your head, walking, and clothing movement. If the external receiver or audio
interface has an input-gain control, lower it there when loud words crackle or
flatten. If it has no gain control, change microphone distance or use a better
matched input rather than expecting the OBS fader to repair clipped capture.

VISP's microphone picker selects the input; it is not a replacement for the
accessory's gain control. The ordinary relay path forwards the phone
contribution into OBS without normalizing or repairing its audio.

In OBS, confirm that exactly one phone source moves in the Audio Mixer and make
a local recording. OBS's official
[Audio Mixer Guide](https://obsproject.com/kb/audio-mixer-guide) recommends
checking levels at the original device and again in OBS, then listening back
before going live. Lowering an OBS fader makes a clipped signal quieter; it
does not make the clipped waveform clean.

If you hear silence, echo, a fixed delay, or only one side of an interface,
continue with the
[phone-camera audio troubleshooting guide](/blog/fix-phone-audio-obs).

## 4. Check return audio after the input changes

An external audio device can affect more than capture. Connecting or
disconnecting a headset, receiver, or interface may also change where the
phone plays chat text-to-speech, alerts, monitoring, or other app audio.
Apple documents plug, unplug, and configuration events as
[audio-route changes](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/routechangenotification).
Android audio capture similarly exposes routing-change notifications through
its AudioRouting API.

Test the complete field experience with headphones where appropriate:

- confirm the intended microphone still drives the VISP meter;
- play one chat text-to-speech message if you use that feature;
- check that return audio does not leak from a speaker into the microphone;
- lock the cable and receiver where normal walking cannot pull them loose;
- verify the accessory's battery and expected operating range;
- repeat the test after one disconnect and reconnect.

Do not assume a successful desk recording proves the roaming setup. Body
position, a pocketed receiver, nearby radio traffic, wind, and cable movement
only appear in a realistic walking test.

## 5. Verify the path you will actually broadcast

The final check differs slightly between an OBS-first and Direct workflow.

### Phone to VISP to OBS to Twitch or Kick

Start the phone contribution, select the matching VISP Media Source in OBS,
and confirm its Audio Mixer meter moves. Record at least 30 seconds with normal
speech, a loud sentence, silence, movement, and a hand clap. Listen on
headphones and ordinary speakers, then check the private or public platform
output from a second device through headphones.

OBS owns the final mix in this path, so it is also where you balance the phone
against music, alerts, a studio host, or other cameras. The external microphone
is already encoded beside the phone video before that source reaches OBS.

### Phone to VISP Direct to Twitch or Kick

OBS is not in the delivery path, so an OBS filter or fader cannot change what
Direct viewers hear. Run a short platform test and listen from a second device
through headphones. Confirm both speech and the wanted environmental sound
before ending the test.

The [VISP phone-app documentation](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/phone-app)
describes both contribution paths. VISP can carry the selected microphone with
the phone camera, but it cannot restore audio that clipped at the accessory or
guarantee that a disconnected input will change routes cleanly during a live
show.

## Plan for a microphone disconnect

Treat an unplugged receiver or dead wireless transmitter as an on-air fault,
not as a seamless input switch. When VISP refreshes settings and a saved input
is no longer available, it clears that unavailable selection back toward the
system default. The operating system still controls the actual route, so stop,
reconnect, reselect, and verify whenever the production allows.

For an OBS production, keep a local fallback scene ready while the field
operator fixes audio. For Direct, the platform feed has no home-OBS layer to
hide the repair, so a tested built-in-microphone fallback matters even more.

Before leaving, rehearse this recovery once:

1. stop the contribution;
2. disconnect the external microphone;
3. select and test **System default**;
4. reconnect the accessory;
5. select it by name again;
6. make one more recording or platform check.

## External-microphone checklist for an IRL stream

Before every Twitch or Kick broadcast:

1. charge the microphone, transmitter, receiver, and phone;
2. secure the windscreen, cable, adapter, and strain relief;
3. connect the accessory before starting capture;
4. select the named microphone in VISP while stopped;
5. prove it with the phone meter and a wrong-input test;
6. test normal and loud speech without clipping;
7. confirm chat TTS or other return audio still goes where intended;
8. record and listen through the real OBS or Direct path;
9. carry a built-in-microphone or spare-accessory recovery plan;
10. repeat a short check at the real location.

An external microphone is useful only when the phone is actually capturing it.
Start with the [VISP getting-started guide](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/get-started),
connect one microphone, and try VISP with a short private test before building
the rest of the production. If the route survives movement, a loud sentence,
and one reconnect, it is ready for a real IRL rehearsal.
