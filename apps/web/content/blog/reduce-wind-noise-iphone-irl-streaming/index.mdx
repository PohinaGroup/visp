Wind can ruin an IRL stream even when the picture and mobile connection are
excellent. The usual mistake is treating every unwanted sound as the same
problem. **Wind hitting a microphone, a loud crowd, steady background hum, and
clipping need different fixes.** One aggressive noise filter cannot repair all
four, and it can make speech sound thin or unstable.

For an iPhone-to-OBS workflow, use this order: stop wind before it reaches the
microphone, choose the right input, apply one appropriate isolation layer, and
judge the result from an OBS recording. That keeps the solution understandable
when you are outside and about to go live on Twitch or Kick.

![Wind is reduced at the microphone, speech is optionally isolated on the iPhone, VISP carries the contribution feed, and OBS provides the final check and optional mild suppression](./wind-noise-audio-path.svg)

## The short answer: fix the earliest bad stage

Start with the sound you actually hear, not the filter you happen to know:

| Symptom | First action |
| --- | --- |
| Low rumble or bursts when air reaches the microphone | Add a correctly fitted windscreen and move the microphone out of direct wind |
| Voice is clear but traffic or a crowd competes with it | Move the microphone closer, then test VISP Audio isolation on iPhone |
| Mild steady noise remains in the home OBS mix | Test one OBS Noise Suppression filter on the phone source |
| Loud words crackle or sound flattened | Reduce input gain or change the microphone position; suppression cannot undo clipping |
| Ambient sound is part of the story | Leave isolation off or use it lightly so the scene still sounds real |
| Audio becomes late after hosted processing | Record a clap test and correct only the measured difference |

This sequence matters because every later stage receives what the earlier stage
captured. If wind overloads the microphone capsule, software only sees a badly
damaged signal. If the microphone is clean and the remaining noise is mild,
software has a much easier job.

## 1. Stop wind at the microphone

A foam cover is useful for breath and light air movement, but an exposed phone
microphone is a poor target in a waterfront gust. Use a windscreen intended for
the microphone you carry and fit it as its manufacturer directs. Shure's
[windscreen guide](https://pubs.shure.com/view/guide/A2WS/en-US.pdf), for
example, describes a windscreen as protection against unwanted breath and wind
noise in outdoor use. The exact accessory differs by microphone; the useful
principle is the physical barrier, not that particular model.

Placement is just as important:

1. Put the microphone near the speaker instead of increasing gain from a
   distance.
2. Keep it out of the direct airflow around a moving hand, bicycle, open car
   window, or waterfront.
3. Stop fabric, straps, and cables from rubbing the microphone or connector.
4. If using a wireless or wired external microphone, confirm the iPhone has
   actually selected it before leaving the studio.
5. Record walking, turning, and a realistic speaking voice. A stationary indoor
   test does not reveal handling or wind problems.

Do not remove every environmental sound by default. Footsteps, a crowd, and a
venue can be useful parts of an IRL broadcast. The aim is intelligible speech
without destructive wind bursts, not studio silence at all times.

## 2. Select the input before enabling isolation

In the VISP iPhone app, stop publishing before changing the microphone. Open
**Settings → Audio**, choose **System default** or the named input you intend to
use, and speak while watching the microphone level.

Disconnect or cover the intended microphone briefly. If the level keeps moving
normally, the phone is probably capturing another input. Correct that before
touching isolation. A clean filter on the wrong microphone is still the wrong
audio.

The native VISP app combines the selected microphone with the H.264 camera feed
and publishes the contribution over SRT. The normal relay path authenticates
and forwards that feed; it does not transcode or normalize the phone audio on
the way into OBS. See the
[phone app documentation](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/phone-app) for the
current capture and publishing workflow.

## 3. Turn on VISP Audio isolation on iPhone

With the stream stopped, open **Settings → Audio** and enable **Audio
isolation**. The switch is currently an iOS feature; it is not shown in the
Android or browser publisher.

VISP then uses the iPhone's native voice-processing path. If iOS opens its
microphone-mode sheet, choose **Voice Isolation**. Apple's current
[Mic Mode guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-us/101993) says Voice
Isolation blocks ambient noise to make speech clearer, while Wide Spectrum
leaves ambient sound unfiltered. Apple also notes that the selected mode is
specific to the app, so a setting used in a phone call should not be assumed to
be active in VISP.

Start a test contribution and record these four samples in OBS:

- ten seconds of the real background without speech;
- normal speech facing the microphone;
- speech while walking and turning;
- one intentionally loud sentence.

Listen for more than reduced noise. Check whether word endings disappear,
whether the voice becomes metallic, and whether wanted venue sound is lost.
Voice isolation is a speech-first choice. Turn it off when the sound of a band,
street performance, crowd, or nature scene is the content rather than noise.

## 4. Know when Better audio isolation is different

Some VISP accounts expose a second **Better audio isolation** switch after
Audio isolation is enabled. It is not merely a stronger setting for the same
local processor.

| Mode | Where processing happens | Practical trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audio isolation | On the iPhone's native audio path | No hosted audio-isolation round trip; best first software test |
| Better audio isolation | Hosted processing through the VISP app host and ElevenLabs | Can remove more complex background sound, but uses extra upload and adds about half a second of delay |
| OBS Noise Suppression | On the home OBS computer after the feed arrives | Useful for mild residual noise in an OBS output; does not clean a VISP Direct output that bypasses OBS |

The hosted VISP path sends short 16 kHz mono PCM chunks through an authenticated
app endpoint to ElevenLabs. ElevenLabs documents its
[Audio Isolation streaming endpoint](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/audio-isolation/stream)
as removing background noise from audio and supports that same raw PCM format.
Because this is external processing, treat it differently from the on-device
option when evaluating latency, uplink use, and your production's privacy
requirements.

Better audio isolation appears only when the feature is enabled for the
account, and the VISP deployment also needs the hosted provider configured.
If the switch is absent, the native option remains the ordinary path; do not
build a show plan around an unavailable flag.

After enabling hosted isolation, repeat the same recording and add a visible
hand clap. The app deliberately buffers roughly half a second of audio for each
processing request, so verify lip sync from the resulting file. Do not copy a
sync offset from another phone or network. Measure this particular device,
microphone, and route.

## 5. Add OBS suppression only for mild residual noise

If the phone contribution sounds good but a mild steady noise remains, OBS can
process the Media Source before the final Twitch or Kick output:

1. In the OBS Audio Mixer, open **Filters** for the VISP phone source.
2. Add **Noise Suppression**.
3. Start with RNNoise and make a short local recording.
4. Compare it with the filter disabled at the same listening volume.
5. Keep the filter only if speech improves without audible pumping or missing
   syllables.

OBS's official
[Noise Suppression Filter guide](https://obsproject.com/kb/noise-suppression-filter)
says the filter is intended for mild background or white noise and is generally
not effective against large amounts of noise such as a loud room. It also warns
that stronger Speex suppression can distort other sounds, including the voice.
That is why an OBS filter belongs after microphone placement, not before it.

Avoid stacking native isolation, hosted isolation, and a strong OBS filter just
because all three are available. Test one change at a time. Multiple speech
processors can each interpret the previous processor's artifacts as noise and
remove more than you intended.

The OBS filter affects only the OBS program. If VISP Direct is sending the
phone contribution to Twitch, Kick, or both without OBS in the delivery path,
an OBS filter cannot change what those viewers hear. Clean the sound at the
iPhone in that workflow. The
[VISP Direct overview](/blog/what-is-visp-direct) explains when the platform
output bypasses OBS.

## 6. Judge the result from a recording, not a meter

A level meter tells you that audio exists and whether peaks approach clipping;
it cannot tell you whether wind masks consonants or isolation damages a voice.
OBS's current
[Audio Mixer Guide](https://obsproject.com/kb/audio-mixer-guide) recommends
checking the signal at the device and again in OBS, then recording and listening
back before going live.

Use headphones and compare files at a similar playback level. A quieter sample
can seem cleaner even when no noise was removed. Include silence, speech, a loud
moment, movement, and a clap in every comparison.

For a remote operator, also verify the public output from a second muted device.
The local OBS recording proves the phone contribution and final mix; the
platform check proves that the intended Twitch or Kick audio track reached
viewers.

## A field checklist for the next IRL stream

Before leaving:

1. fit and secure the correct windscreen;
2. connect the intended microphone and select it in VISP;
3. choose whether environmental sound is content or noise;
4. enable native Audio isolation only when speech should dominate;
5. use Better audio isolation only when available and justified by the test;
6. record a movement, loudness, and clap test in OBS;
7. apply at most one mild OBS suppression filter if needed;
8. save the tested scene and monitoring setup;
9. carry a fallback microphone or be ready to return to the built-in input;
10. repeat a short check at the real location because indoor rehearsal cannot
    reproduce its wind and crowd.

VISP does not create a good microphone signal after the fact, and its normal
relay does not transcode audio or bond network capacity. It gives the iPhone a
managed contribution path into the home studio, where you can inspect and mix
the result before it reaches Twitch or Kick.

If your problem is silence, echo, clipping, or a fixed delay rather than
background noise, use the
[phone-camera audio troubleshooting guide](/blog/fix-phone-audio-obs). For the
complete source setup, follow
[How to Use Your Phone as a Remote Camera in OBS](/blog/use-phone-as-remote-camera-obs)
and the [VISP getting-started guide](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/get-started).
Then try VISP with one test scene and keep the isolation mode that survives a
real outdoor recording, not just a quiet desk test.
