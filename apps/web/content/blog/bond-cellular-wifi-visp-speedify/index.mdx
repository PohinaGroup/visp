To make a VISP phone stream survive one weak connection, **run a
[Speedify](https://speedify.com/irl-streaming-connection-bonding-software/)
bonded tunnel on the field device, then publish into VISP over plain SRT as
usual.** Speedify combines the phone's Wi-Fi and cellular links into one steady
tunnel underneath your encoder, and VISP authenticates the single feed and
sends it on to Twitch, Kick, or YouTube with Direct, or to your own OBS if you
produce there.

You may not need Speedify at all. **The VISP phone app has its own Network
bonding for Wi-Fi plus cellular, and the VISP relay accepts SRTLA from encoders
such as Moblin or BELABOX.** Speedify is the option for everything else: a
system-wide tunnel that bonds the uplink for any encoder, including ones that
cannot bond by themselves. VISP is a self-hosted SRT/RTMP relay and control
plane. It gives each publishing device revocable access and carries the feed
it receives; Speedify works a layer below it.

## VISP Native bonding versus Speedify bonding

The [VISP relay](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/architecture) sits between
publishing devices and the platforms or your OBS. A phone connects outward to
VISP, and with Direct the relay makes one encode per destination, so the phone
never holds a stream key. Each device can be revoked on its own.

In the VISP iOS and Android app, **Settings → Network → Network bonding** uses
Wi-Fi and cellular at the same time. The **Mode** picker offers **SRTLA
(aggregating)**, which spreads the stream across both links; **Broadcast**,
which sends every packet over both links and keeps the first copy (more
redundancy, roughly double the mobile data); and **Main + backup**, which
prefers one link and falls back to the other. Bonding is off until you turn it
on.

There are four common ways to bond, and they live at different layers:

- **In the VISP app**, through Network bonding as above. Nothing else to
  install, but it only covers the phone's own Wi-Fi and cellular links.
- **On a third-party encoder**, through SRTLA. Apps like
  [Moblin](/blog/moblin-remote-camera-obs-srt) and BELABOX can spread one SRT
  stream across several links. Per the
  [BELABOX SRTLA project](https://github.com/BELABOX/srtla) this needs a
  matching `srtla_rec` receiver to reassemble the links, and the VISP relay
  runs one. Turn on **Show RTMP and SRTLA fallback URLs** in the dashboard
  **Devices** tab to copy a device's SRTLA URL.
- **In dedicated hardware**, such as
  [LiveU Solo](https://solohelp.liveu.tv/hc/en-us/articles/16672822061339-Overview-of-the-Solo-PRO),
  which pairs an encoder with a commercial bonding service. That trade-off is
  covered in [VISP vs BELABOX vs LiveU Solo](/blog/visp-vs-belabox-vs-liveu-solo).
- **Below every app**, through a system-level bonding VPN like Speedify. This
  is the approach that lets you keep your existing VISP encoder unchanged and
  still bond the uplink.

Speedify is attractive because it is app-agnostic. You do not have to switch
encoders; you install it on the field device and every stream that leaves the
phone, including your VISP SRT feed, rides the bonded tunnel. It can also bond
links a phone app cannot see, such as Ethernet or satellite on a laptop
encoder. If you run Speedify, leave VISP's Network bonding off so you are
testing one bonding layer at a time.

## How Speedify fills the gap

Speedify describes itself as connection bonding software that "combines Wi-Fi,
4G / 5G cellular, Ethernet, Starlink and other satellite internet connections
together" and "works with all live streaming software and social platforms." On
its [channel bonding page](https://speedify.com/features/channel-bonding/) it
explains the mechanism: it "breaks your traffic into packets and sends them
across every connection at once," so "to everything you use, it looks like one
fast, steady connection." Crucially, that bonding happens at the connection
level, transparently, without per-app configuration.

That last point is what makes it work with VISP. SRT still runs end to end
between your encoder and the VISP relay; Speedify simply carries that traffic
over a bonded, encrypted tunnel and reassembles it at its own Speed Server
before it egresses to VISP. Your encoder and VISP both see one connection.
Speedify also ships two reliability behaviors worth knowing: a **Redundant
Mode** that "sends every packet over every connection at the same time and keeps
whichever copy lands first," and a default **Streaming Mode** that automatically
switches real-time traffic between bonding for speed and duplicating for
reliability. Speedify installs on iPhone, iPad, Android, Windows, macOS, and
Linux, so it can run on the same phone that publishes to VISP.

## What you need

- A VISP account signed in with Twitch, Kick, or Google
- Your usual VISP publisher on the phone: the native app, or an encoder like
  [Larix Broadcaster](/blog/larix-broadcaster-obs-srt) or Moblin
- Speedify installed on that same field device, with at least two working
  connections (for example Wi-Fi plus cellular, or two cellular modems)
- A plan for a total outage: the optional BRB card with Direct, or a local
  fallback scene if you produce in OBS
- Optional: OBS Studio on a production computer
- Headphones for checking audio without creating a return-path echo

Rehearse with the same phone, carriers, route, and time of day you expect to use
live. Bonding two connections that are both congested at the same spot does not
create bandwidth, and a fast Wi-Fi test at home never validates a moving
cellular production.

## 1. Install and start Speedify on the field device

Install Speedify on the phone, sign in, and confirm it shows two or more active
connections. On a phone that usually means Wi-Fi plus cellular; enabling both
radios at once is what gives Speedify something to bond. Because Speedify runs as
a system-level tunnel, you do not point your encoder at it — once it is active,
the VISP SRT traffic already flows through it.

## 2. Choose the right bonding mode

Leave Speedify in its default **Streaming Mode** for most live shows; it is
built for real-time traffic and adapts per stream. If you are crossing an area
where you expect frequent brief drops and can spare the extra data, **Redundant
Mode** sends a copy over every link so a single dropped path does not interrupt
the feed. Redundant Mode trades bandwidth for reliability, so test it on the real
route at the bitrate you actually stream.

## 3. Publish into VISP over plain SRT

Nothing changes in your VISP setup. With the VISP app, sign in and go live as
usual; it creates its device for you. With a third-party encoder, open the
dashboard **Devices** tab, choose **Add device** under **Video sources**, and
name it something practical like "roaming phone." Copy the `srt://` sending URL
and paste it into your encoder, as in the
[phone-as-a-remote-camera guide](/blog/use-phone-as-remote-camera-obs). Treat the
whole URL as a password. Keep SRT selected unless the network blocks UDP; turn
on **Show RTMP and SRTLA fallback URLs** for an RTMP URL, and the
[video-source docs](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/video-source) show the
exact dashboard flow.

Use H.264 video and AAC audio. With Direct, the relay re-encodes for each
platform but can only work with the picture that arrives; in the optional
relay-to-OBS path it does not transcode at all, so the codec, resolution,
frame rate, and bitrate your encoder sends are what OBS receives. Choose a bitrate the bonded connection can sustain with margin, not a
single speed-test peak. A stable 720p30 picture beats a nominal 1080p feed that
constantly queues.

## 4. Set SRT latency for the bonded path

SRT can retransmit lost UDP packets while they are still useful, and its latency
window gives those retransmissions time to arrive. The
[SRT project](https://github.com/Haivision/srt) documents this automatic-repeat
behavior, and the
[SRT Alliance overview](https://www.srtalliance.org/about-srt-technology/)
explains why the latency window is tied to round-trip time. Speedify adds a Speed
Server hop and its own reassembly, so the effective round-trip can be a little
longer than a direct cellular path. Third-party encoders take the SRT latency
from their own settings (the VISP app uses a fixed window). Run VISP's latency
probe from the dashboard **Advanced** tab on the connection you will actually
use and choose the cellular profile, then keep some extra
headroom rather than shrinking the value to advertise a smaller delay. VISP turns
the median of seven round-trip samples into starting recommendations:

| Profile | Recommendation | Minimum |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Wired | 3× measured RTT | 120 ms |
| Wi-Fi | 4× measured RTT | 300 ms |
| Cellular | 6× measured RTT | 600 ms |

The [OBS SRT guide](https://obsproject.com/kb/srt-protocol-streaming-guide)
describes latency the same way; VISP simply applies more conservative floors
tuned for mobile contribution. For the encoder-side settings, see the
[broadcaster setup docs](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/broadcaster-setup).

## 5. Check the output and rehearse recovery

With Direct, watch the dashboard **Home** tab preview or a private test on the
platform. If you also produce in OBS (optional), your encoder uses the
**sending URL** and OBS uses the separate **OBS read URL** from the
**Destinations** tab. The quickest OBS setup is to pair the VISP plugin, select
the device, and add it to the current scene, as described in
[VISP OBS remote control](https://docs.visp-stream.com/docs/obs-remote-control).
That creates the authenticated Media Source without exposing a public control
port. You can also download the generated scene collection, which includes a
Fallback scene and one reconnecting Media Source per device.

Then run a short failure drill before a real broadcast:

1. Confirm motion and audio for several minutes.
2. Turn off one of the phone's connections (drop Wi-Fi, keep cellular).
3. Watch the feed continue on the remaining bonded link.
4. Restore the connection and confirm Speedify re-adds it.
5. Now disable both connections and confirm the BRB card (Direct) or the OBS
   fallback scene appears.
6. Restore the network and wait for stable playback before cutting back.

Steps 2–4 prove the bonding is doing its job; steps 5–6 prove that even bonding
cannot beat a total outage, which is why you keep a fallback. For a
production that should keep entertaining viewers during any interruption, follow
the [mobile-network resilience guide](/blog/keep-stream-live-bad-mobile-network).

## Speedify vs on-encoder bonding vs hardware

Speedify is the least intrusive option: it rides under any VISP encoder and
bonds whatever links the device has. Its trade-offs are a subscription, an extra
Speed Server hop, and dependence on Speedify's servers for the reassembly.
VISP's own Network bonding and on-encoder **SRTLA** through Moblin or BELABOX
bond the SRT stream itself with no third-party service; the VISP relay is the
SRTLA receiver. They only bond the links that encoder can reach. Dedicated
hardware like LiveU Solo bundles the encoder and a bonding service into one
supported appliance at a higher cost. Choose Speedify when you want to keep your
current encoder and just harden the uplink; choose VISP's bonding, SRTLA, or
hardware when you need bonding that does not depend on a consumer VPN service.

## What this setup does not do

No bonding layer can invent bandwidth. If both links are saturated or dead in
the same spot, no bonding mode will keep the stream alive, and that is when the
BRB card or OBS fallback scene earns its place. Only one publisher can own a
VISP device path at a time, so give every phone its own device; the [multi-phone IRL guide](/blog/multi-phone-irl-stream-obs)
shows how those independent sources compose. Speedify is also not part of VISP,
is not required for VISP, and is not endorsed by it — it is one third-party layer
that happens to fit below the relay.

## Troubleshooting

### Bonding is on, but the stream still stutters

Confirm both connections are genuinely active in Speedify and are not congested
at the same location. Lower the encoder bitrate, raise the SRT latency window,
and re-run VISP's latency probe. Bonding two weak links does not add up to one
strong one when both degrade together.

### VISP never shows Live

Check that the full current `srt://` publishing URL is pasted, that no other
encoder owns that device path, and that UDP is not blocked. If the credential
was rotated, replace the old URL. This is unrelated to Speedify.

### VISP is Live, but OBS is blank

This only applies if you use OBS. Make sure OBS has the OBS read URL, not the
sending URL, with **Local File**
disabled, and wait for a keyframe. If the OBS read credential was rotated,
refresh the source.

### Latency feels high

Speedify's Speed Server adds a hop. Pick a nearby server if the app lets you,
keep Streaming Mode rather than Redundant Mode when data allows, and accept that
a bonded, reassembled path trades a little delay for resilience.

## Pre-stream checklist

- Speedify shows two or more active connections and the intended mode.
- VISP's own Network bonding is off while Speedify runs.
- The encoder publishes the current VISP `srt://` URL over plain SRT.
- H.264, AAC, orientation, keyframe interval, and bitrate are tested on the route.
- SRT latency comes from a probe on the real bonded connection.
- The Direct output reports Live, or OBS (if used) receives picture and audio.
- Dropping one link keeps the feed alive; dropping both shows the BRB card or
  fallback scene.
- No stream key is typed into the phone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Speedify replace VISP?

No. Speedify is a network layer that bonds connections; VISP is the relay and
control plane that authenticates devices and sends the feed to the platforms
or your OBS. They solve different
problems and work together — Speedify hardens the uplink, VISP manages the feed.

### Do I need to configure my encoder to use Speedify?

No. Speedify bonds at the connection level, so once it is running on the phone,
your VISP SRT traffic already flows through it. You publish into VISP exactly as
you would without it.

### Is Speedify the only way to bond for VISP?

No. The VISP app has built-in Network bonding, Moblin or BELABOX can send
SRTLA to the VISP relay's SRTLA URL, and dedicated bonded hardware is another
route. Speedify is the option that works under any encoder without changing it.

### Will bonding stop my stream from ever dropping?

No. Bonding and SRT recovery ride out brief interruptions and single-link
failures, but neither creates bandwidth. Keep a BRB card or OBS fallback scene
for a total outage.
